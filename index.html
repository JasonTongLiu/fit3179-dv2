Australian road safety

The Road Ahead

By Jason Tong Liu · Oct 28, 2026

Australia cut its road toll dramatically over three decades. Yet 1,310 people died in 2025—more than twice the 2030 target. This story follows the slowdown from the national trend to the places, times, and road users carrying the burden.

01

A long decline, then slower progress

Start with the national pattern and compare recent progress with the 2030 target.

Australia recorded 2,800 road deaths in 1989. By 2025, that total had fallen to 1,310, while the population-adjusted fatality rate declined from 16.5 to 4.7 deaths per 100,000 people.

The long-term improvement is substantial, but the recent path is less reassuring. The 2025 total remains more than twice the 2030 target of about 570 deaths. The 2026 point covers January to July only, so it is shown for recency rather than used as a full-year comparison.

The national curve raises the next question: if progress has slowed, where is the remaining burden concentrated?

Risk depends on where the road runs

Population-adjusted state rates, regional totals, and age patterns reveal geographic differences.

The national average conceals a wide geographic gap. In 2025, the Northern Territory recorded 14.2 deaths per 100,000 people, compared with 1.8 in the Australian Capital Territory. Using rates here matters because state populations differ greatly.

At SA4 level, the map switches to totals: Wide Bay and Western Australia’s Wheat Belt recorded the largest 2021–2025 counts in this dataset. These circles show where deaths accumulated, not where an individual faced the highest risk. The age distributions below add another layer by showing how the profile changes with remoteness.

Place explains part of the pattern. The next chapter moves from where deaths occur to when they happen and who is involved.

05

Conclusion: progress needs a more targeted next step

The long decline is real, but it does not guarantee that Australia will reach the 2030 target.

Australia’s road-safety story is one of major long-term improvement followed by slower recent progress. A national total of 1,310 deaths in 2025 remains far above the 2030 target, so repeating the pace of the last few years will not be enough.

The evidence points toward targeted action rather than a single national solution: compare places using population-adjusted rates, investigate regional clusters without confusing totals with risk, and focus prevention on the road users, times, and crash contexts where deaths concentrate.

International comparisons show that lower fatality rates are achievable even in highly motorised countries. The road ahead therefore depends on combining safer infrastructure, safer speeds, safer vehicles, and safer behaviour—and measuring progress with complete, comparable data.

The central finding is therefore not that Australia has failed to improve. It is that past success has made the unfinished task more visible: the remaining deaths are unevenly distributed, and the next gains will require attention to those differences.
