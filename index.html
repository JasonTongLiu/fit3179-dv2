Australian road safety

The Road Ahead

Australia's roads are much safer than they were three decades ago, but recent progress is uneven. The following charts use only the latest downloaded records—no forecast values are added.

01

A long decline, then slower progress

Start with the national pattern and compare the latest complete year with the 2030 target.

Australia recorded 2,800 road deaths in 1989. By 2025, that total had fallen to 1,310, while the population-adjusted fatality rate declined from 16.5 to 4.7 deaths per 100,000 people.

The long-term improvement is substantial, but the recent path is less reassuring. The 2025 total remains more than twice the 2030 target of about 570 deaths. The 2026 point covers January to July only, so it is shown for recency rather than used as a full-year comparison.

Fatalities and fatality rate, 1989–2026

Both measures show the same long-term improvement, but the annual count has moved away from its 2020 low and reached 1,310 in 2025. The rate remains much lower than in 1989 because the number of deaths fell while Australia's population grew.

How to read: Use the left axis for navy fatalities and the right axis for the teal rate. Compare the direction of the two lines rather than their vertical distance. The coral 2026 point covers January–July only.

Source: BITRE Australian Road Deaths Database; ABS national population (December observations).

Progress toward the 2030 target

Australia recorded 1,310 deaths in 2025, above the 2018–2020 baseline average of 1,139 and more than twice the 2030 target of about 570. This chart measures the size of the remaining gap; it does not predict the 2030 result.

02

Risk depends on where the road runs

Population-adjusted state rates, regional totals, and age patterns reveal geographic differences.

The national average conceals a wide geographic gap. In 2025, the Northern Territory recorded 14.2 deaths per 100,000 people, compared with 1.8 in the Australian Capital Territory. Using rates here matters because state populations differ greatly.

At SA4 level, the map switches to totals: Wide Bay and Western Australia’s Wheat Belt recorded the largest 2021–2025 counts in this dataset. These circles show where deaths accumulated, not where an individual faced the highest risk. The age distributions below add another layer by showing how the profile changes with remoteness.

Fatality rate by state and territory

In 2025, the Northern Territory had the highest rate at 14.2 deaths per 100,000 people, while the Australian Capital Territory had the lowest at 1.8. Rates make differently sized populations more comparable.

How to read: Darker teal means a higher fatality rate, not a larger number of deaths.

Source: BITRE ARDD; ABS population; ABS boundaries.

Fatalities by SA4 region

Wide Bay recorded 209 deaths and Western Australia's Wheat Belt recorded 205 from 2021–2025, the two largest SA4 totals in the data. Coastal clusters partly reflect where people and road travel are concentrated.

How to read: Circle area represents fatalities, so compare the area rather than the radius. Overlaps indicate nearby SA4 regions; these are totals, not population-adjusted rates.

Source: BITRE ARDD; ABS SA4 boundaries.

Age profile by remoteness

The peaks and spreads change across remoteness categories, showing that the age profile of fatalities is not identical everywhere. This supports treating remoteness as part of the road-safety context rather than assuming one national age pattern.

How to read: Compare where each coloured ridge peaks and how widely it spreads. Ridge height represents density—the shape of the age distribution—not the number of deaths.

03

Time, road users, and crash context

Three views of the recorded conditions associated with fatalities from 2021–2025.

Fatalities are not evenly distributed across the week. The largest day–hour cell is Friday at 3 pm, with 70 recorded deaths from 2021–2025, while several other peaks occur on Thursday, Friday, and Sunday afternoons.

Drivers form the largest road-user group in the data, and drivers aged 40–64 form the largest road-user–age cell. The alluvial view then follows these records through crash type and speed band, showing that road-user risk is connected to the context in which a crash occurs.

04

Australia in international context

Compare reported outcomes with vehicle ownership and peer-country rankings.

The WHO indicators place Australia at 4.5 road deaths per 100,000 people, despite a high level of registered vehicle ownership. This suggests that vehicle numbers alone do not determine road-safety outcomes; road design, travel patterns, enforcement, and policy also matter.

Among the nine selected peer countries, Australia ranked sixth in 2023 when first place represents the lowest fatality rate. Country reporting years and definitions can vary, so the comparison is best read as international context rather than a perfect league table.

Road fatality rates worldwide

Australia's reported rate of 4.5 deaths per 100,000 people sits toward the lower end of the global distribution while its registered vehicle ownership is high. Across countries, the map does not show a simple one-factor relationship between vehicle numbers and road deaths.

How to read: Each fixed-size dot represents a country and colour shows its fatality rate. Hover over a dot to see the country and its reported registered vehicles per 100,000 people.

Source: WHO GSRRS 2023 indicators; Natural Earth World Atlas. ISO3 codes are joined through a numeric-ID crosswalk.

Australia among selected peer countries

Australia ranked sixth of the nine selected peers in 2023. Norway, Sweden, the United Kingdom, Japan, and Germany recorded lower rates, showing that Australia's long-term improvement still leaves room for further gains.

How to read: Rank 1 is the lowest fatality rate. Follow each country's colour across time; a rising line means its relative position worsened even if its own rate may have fallen.

Source: OECD/ITF annual road-safety data and OECD peer population, 1990–2023.

05

Conclusion: progress needs a more targeted next step

The long decline is real, but it does not guarantee that Australia will reach the 2030 target.

Australia’s road-safety story is one of major long-term improvement followed by slower recent progress. A national total of 1,310 deaths in 2025 remains far above the 2030 target, so repeating the pace of the last few years will not be enough.

The evidence points toward targeted action rather than a single national solution: compare places using population-adjusted rates, investigate regional clusters without confusing totals with risk, and focus prevention on the road users, times, and crash contexts where deaths concentrate.

International comparisons show that lower fatality rates are achievable even in highly motorised countries. The road ahead therefore depends on combining safer infrastructure, safer speeds, safer vehicles, and safer behaviour—and measuring progress with complete, comparable data.
